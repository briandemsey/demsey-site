Feet Firmly On The Ground

An Evening with Brian Demsey — a ninety-minute one-man keynote, Version 1 (warm / universal), Draft 3

By Brian Demsey

← Back to Articles
Production note

This is a performance draft, built to be spoken, not read silently. The short paragraphs are breath units, and the italic lines in brackets are delivery cues, not lines to say aloud. Running time is roughly ninety minutes at an unhurried pace, with the laughs and the silences doing the rest of the work. Every quotation drawn from the essays is marked so it can be checked word-for-word against the original before anything reaches a teleprompter; a few specific names and figures are bracketed for the same reason. Nothing here should be spoken in public until you have confirmed it is exactly true.

Movement One: What Do You See?

The cold open — ~7 minutes

[Walk to the microphone slowly. Let them watch you do it. Say nothing until you arrive.]

Good evening.

Let me begin by telling you exactly how much money I made last year on the great work of my life.

Two and a half years I gave it. A real product. A working website. An app, the kind that lives on the phone in your pocket, that you can hold in your hand. I poured everything I had into it, and everything I am.

Do you know what it earned?

Not enough for a tank of gas.

[Let that sit. Don’t rescue it.]

Now. You’ve already decided what kind of evening this is going to be. I can feel it from up here. It’s the sad one. The old-man-who-tried one. The gentle little talk where the nice elderly gentleman shares his disappointments and we all clap kindly and go home feeling grateful for our own careers.

So let me save us both the next eighty-five minutes of that.

That failure was the best investment I have ever made. And I want you to understand. I have made a few good ones. I have signed my name to numbers that moved real money. I am not a man who confuses losing with winning to make himself feel better at breakfast.

I am telling you, with a clear and professional eye, that the worst financial decision of my life was the best decision of my life.

Because I was never really building a company.

I found that out the way you find out most true things, too late to do anything but learn from it. What I was actually building, across those two and a half years, at an age when the world had quietly filed me under finished, was a new version of myself.

At eighty-three, I taught myself to conduct the most powerful instruments human beings have ever pointed at the act of thinking. Not to poke at them. Not to dabble. To orchestrate them. To make eight artificial minds answer the same question at the same moment, and then to sit in judgment over which of them was lying to my face.

I lost the investment. I kept the education.

And I promise you, from here, from this vantage point, with the years I have left in plain view, that is the better half of the trade.

[Beat. Shift. Warmer now.]

So look at me again. Go ahead. What do you actually see?

A frail old man. Be honest. It’s a reasonable guess. I’m eighty-three. I have lost more friends than I would like to count. I have run twenty-two marathons and my knees have filed a formal, notarized complaint about every single one of them. When I walked to this microphone just now, some quiet part of you did the arithmetic we all do, the arithmetic none of us admits to, the sum that says: this one is near the end of his usefulness.

I want to take that sum apart tonight. Because I think it is the most expensive miscalculation our whole civilization is making, and I think we make it about our elders and about ourselves, and I think it is wrong the way a confident, well-dressed, badly-figured actuarial table is wrong, which is to say, wrong in a way that costs people their futures.

Here is what eighty-three years actually is. It is not a countdown. It is a library. It is five decades of standing in rooms where being wrong had a price. Real rooms, with real people, who were going to live or die financially by the number I put my name beneath. I did not learn caution in a seminar. I learned it from consequence. And that is the one thing in this entire accelerating world that you still cannot download, cannot fake, and cannot rush. You can only reason your way into it.

That’s what I have that’s worth something. Not the app. Not the website. The reason.

So here is my promise for the next ninety minutes, and then I’ll get out of the way of my own story. I am going to tell you the truth about a machine that lies. I am going to tell you the truth about a body that is falling apart on schedule. I am going to tell you a secret my family kept for three generations. And somewhere in there I am going to try to convince you of the one thing I now believe more than anything else I have ever believed.

The story this country tells about growing old is a lie, a lie nearly as confident as anything a machine ever invented. The lie says the light dims. My experience is the exact opposite. The noise falls away. The vanity falls away. The terror of what other people think of you: that falls away too, and good riddance. And what is left, when all of that finally burns off, is clarity.

I see more clearly at eighty-three than I did at forty-three. (Of course, two cataract lenses repaved with plastic were a tad helpful, too.)

And I was nobody’s fool at forty-three.

[Simple. No flourish.]

Don’t lament. Engage.

Let me tell you what that means to me.

Movement Two: Pricing the Truth

Who I am, and the craft I bring — ~12 minutes

I should tell you what I did for a living, because it explains everything that comes after, including why an eighty-three-year-old ended up building a lie-detector for robots.

I was an actuary.

[Wait for it. Somebody always laughs.]

I know. Try saying that at a cocktail party and watch the crowd part like the Red Sea. An actuary is the person who prices risk, who sits with the cold numbers and works out how likely it is that the thing you’re afraid of actually happens, and what it costs when it does.

We are the people the insurance company sends into the back room to do the math nobody else wants to do. We are, if I’m honest, professional pessimists. We are paid by the hour to measure the exact distance between what people hope is true and what actually is.

For fifty years, that was my job. And here is what fifty years of that does to your mind. It makes you allergic, physically almost, to confidence without evidence, more commonly known as bullshit. I spent my whole working life learning that the most dangerous sentence in any room is not “I don’t know.” The most dangerous sentence is a wrong answer delivered with a firm handshake, dressed in a good suit.

Now hold that thought. Because I want to take you back to the very first time I ever had to stand up and speak in public. I was thirteen years old.

[Smile at the memory.]

Thirteen. And I got up in front of the room, and I opened my mouth, and my legs, my legs began to shake. Not a tremble. A visible, architectural shaking, the kind the people in the front row can see and are too polite to mention. I was having a small private skirmish with gravity, up there, and the whole audience decided, mercifully, to pretend they couldn’t see it.

And I learned something at thirteen that took me fifty more years to fully understand. I learned that the audience can always tell. They can tell when your legs are shaking. And more importantly, they can tell when you’re lying, even when you can’t. There is a kind of truth that a body leaks whether you want it to or not. By the way, did you catch the pun?

Machines don’t have that.

That’s the whole problem. Let me tell you when I first understood it.

It was late 2023. I sat down, an old man, curious, in front of one of these new artificial intelligences that everyone was suddenly talking about. And I asked it things. And it answered me. Beautifully. Flawless grammar. Total poise. The confidence of a tenured professor and the warmth of a friend.

And some of what it told me was completely, utterly wrong. Completely fabricated.

Machines look at the alphabet and the math. Write down these words: “I never claimed to be Jewish. I am Catholic. Because I learned my maternal family had a Jewish background, I said I was ‘Jew-ish.’” Of course, the infamous George Santos. The calculus, go back far enough and we’re all ‘ish.

So. Completely, utterly wrong. Completely fabricated. Not hedged. Not “I think.” Not “you may want to check.” It looked me straight in the eye, fifty years of watching for the shaking legs, and this thing had no legs to shake, and it handed me a fabrication with the exact same serene confidence it used to hand me a fact. It could not tell the difference. And worse, it could not tell me that it couldn’t tell the difference.

For most people, that’s a curiosity. A funny story. “Ha, the computer made something up.”

For an actuary, it was a five-alarm fire.

Because I have spent my life on exactly this, on the difference between right and almost right, and I know what that difference costs when it scales. I know that when you put a system that lies with confidence at the center of medicine, of law, of money, of the machinery a civilization runs on. You are not building a productivity tool. You are building a beautifully-worded time bomb.

And that, my friends, is age today. Not the age of the atom bomb, but the age of the time bomb.

So I did what I always did. I reached for the oldest tool in my kit. Triangulation.

Here’s what that means, and here’s the metaphor I keep coming back to. I think about the way this industry moves. Every few weeks, another company rolls out another model, bigger, faster, more confident than the last. And I think of Evel Knievel.

[A little wonder in it.]

You remember Evel Knievel. The white leather jumpsuit, the stars and stripes, the motorcycle, the ramp. He jumped fourteen buses. Fourteen. And the whole world held its breath and watched, because we all understood that the entire spectacle depended on one thing: there is no margin for error. Clear the buses, or don’t.

The artificial intelligence industry, as I wrote, “is adding buses at an astonishing rate.” Every launch, another bus on the pile. Bigger jump, louder crowd, more confidence. And nobody, nobody, is standing at the edge of the ramp asking the actuary’s question, the only question that matters: what is the margin of error, and who lands on the buses when we’re wrong?

Here was a system, I wrote, “that delivered answers with extraordinary confidence and zero margin of error disclosure.”

Zero. An entire industry built on a stunt with no disclosed failure rate.

So my answer, the whole idea, the thing I gave two and a half years and a tank of gas to, was almost embarrassingly simple. If you want to know whether an answer is reliable, “don’t ask one oracle. Ask eight.” Ask eight of these machines the same question, at the same instant, and watch what they do. When they agree, you’ve learned something. And when eight independently-built minds all dodge the same question in the same way: that uniformity is itself the data point. The silence tells you where the bodies are buried.

That’s it. That’s the great invention. An old actuary, refusing to trust a single confident voice, the way he refused to trust one for fifty years.

Now. I’ll be the first to tell you I wasn’t only worried about the machines lying. I was worried about all of us believing them, all at once, with real money.

I wrote a piece. I called it after Cassandra, the Greek prophetess cursed to see the future and never be believed. Also, as it happens, the name of one of my granddaughters, “for the wisdom to see what others miss.” And in it I did the only thing I really know how to do. I priced it. Wall Street, I wrote, “is currently pricing ninety percent probability” that the best possible version of this AI future simply comes true, on schedule, no correction, no reckoning.

Ninety percent.

[Flat, actuarial, deadly.]

“That’s not analysis. That’s hope.”

I’ve been doing this for fifty years. I can tell you the precise difference between analysis and hope, because pricing that difference was my literal job, and I will tell you what I told them: “the gap between hope and reality is where bubbles live.” That gap is not a mood. It is a number. And right now it is enormous.

So that’s who’s standing in front of you. Not a technologist. Not a prophet. An old man with a very particular and very unfashionable skill: I know how to price uncertainty, and I am no longer afraid of anyone in the room who would rather I didn’t.

Which brings me to the machine itself. And to what it’s actually doing to us while it smiles.

Movement Three: The Machine That Lies

The public stakes, and a confession — ~13 minutes

Let me tell you what a hallucination actually is, because the word is doing a lot of gentle, dishonest work.

“Hallucination” sounds whimsical. It sounds like the machine had a little dream, a harmless flight of fancy, bless its heart. The industry loves that word precisely because it sounds harmless.

Here is the plain version. A hallucination is when the system “generates information that appears accurate but is factually incorrect, unverifiable, or fabricated.” In English: it makes something up, and it presents the thing it made up with exactly the same confidence it presents the truth. It cannot flag the difference. It does not experience a difference.

And here is the part that should keep you up at night, the part I keep trying to get people to feel: “as model capabilities increase, incorrect outputs become harder to detect.” Read that again slowly. The smarter these things get, the better their lies get dressed. We are not building our way out of this problem. We are building better-tailored suits for it.

I’ll give you the homeliest way I ever found to say it. I’ve raised dogs. I love dogs. And a puppy, a puppy will fail you honestly. A puppy makes a mess on your carpet and you know. There is evidence. There is, forgive me, a smell. “At least with a puppy you can smell a mess.”

“With large language models, the failures hide in plain sight.”

No smell. No stain. Just a clean, confident, beautifully-formatted paragraph that happens to be false, sitting quietly in the middle of ten true ones, waiting for you to build something on top of it.

I know this personally. I have spent, and I mean this literally, hundreds of hours cleaning up after these machines. Debugging functions they invented that never existed. Restructuring whole plans built on a false premise the machine stated with total authority. Hunting for problems it swore were there and never were. Two and a half years, remember. I didn’t read about this. I lived inside it.

And that’s when I noticed the thing that still makes me angriest.

The business model.

[Let the anger be cold, not hot.]

When one of these machines fails you, when it hallucinates, when it sends you down a false path, what do you do? You ask it again. You re-run it. You add more words, more queries, more attempts to fix what it broke. And every one of those, every re-run, every correction, every cleanup, is another transaction. Another charge. More usage. More revenue.

I wrote it as plainly as I know how: “It’s a business model where failure is literally more profitable than success.”

Sit with that. In almost every honest business ever built, you make money when the thing works. Here, in this one, they make more money when it doesn’t. “At least when a restaurant serves you a bad meal, they might comp your dessert.” These machines serve you a bad meal and then charge you for the three more you order trying to get a good one.

That is not a productivity revolution. In my professional opinion, priced coldly, it is one of the largest transfers of wealth in history from the people doing the work to the people who own the machines. And it is wearing a lab coat and calling itself progress.

[Change of energy. Quieter. More intimate.]

Now. Why does an eighty-three-year-old care this much? Let me show you, and I have to take you out of the boardroom to do it. I have to take you to a road.

I live in South Dakota. On a hill, in a small town, with a lot of sky. And out there, driving those roads, you learn something the machine can never learn.

I’m driving. And a deer steps into the road.

You know what happens. Every one of you who’s driven a country road at dusk knows exactly what happens in your body. “My foot moves before my mind finishes the sentence.” The brake is already down before the thought “there is a deer” has even finished forming. Something older than language, older than reason, something alive, takes the wheel for a half-second and saves your life.

That is the thing the machine does not have. That is the thing I have been trying to name this whole time. “These machines read the past with superhuman fluency. They cannot see the future.” They are, at their core, a rear-view mirror. A magnificent, encyclopedic, superhumanly detailed rear-view mirror. And you cannot drive a civilization forward by staring into the mirror, no matter how good the mirror gets.

When the deer steps into the road, the machine has nothing. No foot on the brake. Only a very confident essay about deer.

So I built the thing that checks. I built the thing that asks eight minds instead of one, that treats confidence as a suspect rather than a witness. And I gave it everything, and it earned me, you remember, not enough for a tank of gas.

I want to tell you why I’m at peace with that. And to do it I have to tell you about a bear.

[The polar bear. Take your time. This is a keystone.]

Years ago, in my actuarial life, I went to present a report at a private mansion. A great fortune lived in that house. And in the front hall, the very first thing you saw when the door opened, was a polar bear. A real one. Twelve feet tall, reared up on its hind legs, killed and stuffed and posed mid-roar, forever, so that everyone who entered would understand, immediately, wordlessly: look what I can afford. Look what I can end.

I have thought about that bear for thirty years.

Because the man who owned it had everything I was supposedly there to help him protect. And standing in that hall, a working actuary with my report under my arm, I realized I had something he had traded away without ever noticing the transaction.

“I was always home for dinner.”

That’s it. That’s the whole thing. Through all of it, all the building and the traveling and the work. “Our travel was as a family of five.” I never mounted a bear in my front hall. I lost the money on Hallucinations dot cloud, and I would lose it again, because “the polar bear is just a trophy. The mansion is just a building. The billion-dollar valuation is just a number.” And a family at the dinner table is not.

I built a machine to protect the truth, and it made me nothing, and I am the richest man I know. That is not a consolation. That is the actual arithmetic, done by a man who spent fifty years doing arithmetic.

“Trust,” I wrote, “is becoming infrastructure.” In the world we are building, the ability to know what is true will be as fundamental as roads, as water, as power. I gave the last chapter of my working life to building a little piece of that infrastructure. The market didn’t pay me for it.

I would still rather have built it than the bear.

Movement Four: Sex or Pickleball

The body, mortality, and a laugh we need — ~10 minutes

[Lighten everything. You’ve earned a breath, and so have they.]

All right. That got heavy. Let me tell you about my body, because my body is a comedy, and I think we could all use the laugh.

I am eighty-three years old. Let me give you the inventory. I have twelve stents holding my heart’s plumbing open. Twelve. I had my first heart attack at fifty-nine, which means I have now been living on borrowed time for nearly a quarter of a century, and I have to say, the interest rate has been very reasonable. I’ve had my lenses replaced. My knees have opinions. Various other parts have filed their own paperwork.

And I have discovered, in my eighties, that there is a particular sentence doctors say to you when you get to be my age. You’ve heard it if you’re my age, or you will. You go in wanting to actually do something, take the medication, have the procedure, live a little, and the doctor gets a certain look. A gentle, patient, faintly funeral look. And he says:

[Do the doctor’s voice: kind, condescending, maddening.]

“At your age… the side effects outweigh the benefits.”

[Straight to the audience.]

At your age. As though the goal at my age is to arrive at death in mint condition. As though the objective is to be a well-preserved corpse. No side effects, no risks, no living, thank you very much.

I wrote an essay about this. I gave it a title I’m rather proud of. I called it “Sex or Pickleball.”

[Let them laugh. Wait.]

Because those, apparently, are the two great dangers facing a man my age. Both, I’m reliably informed by the actuarial tables I used to write, carry a small but real risk of sending an eighty-three-year-old to his reward. And the medical establishment’s considered advice is: perhaps, at your age, neither.

To which I say: what, exactly, am I preserving myself for?

Here is the truth I found underneath the joke, and it’s the only thing in this movement I’m dead serious about. “The cautionary tale isn’t about choosing between pleasures. It’s about the danger of stopping altogether.” The thing that kills you is not the pickleball. The thing that kills you is the couch. It is the surrender. It is the slow, sensible, well-advised decision to stop. To stop moving, stop wanting, stop risking, stop engaging, dressed up as prudence.

I have run twenty-two marathons. I have paddled, alone, across the open ocean, the Catalina Channel, the Molokai Channel, water that does not care one bit whether you make it or not. And I did not do those things because I was fearless. I did them because I had decided, somewhere along the way, that I would rather wear out than rust out.

[Now, the misdiagnosis. Turn from funny to pointed without losing warmth.]

But let me tell you the story that ties this whole night together, because it happened right here, in this body, in this town, and it is the machine’s failure wearing a white coat.

A couple of years ago, I was eighty-one, I started to notice something. A tremor. A slowness. And I went to a neurologist. And the neurologist examined me, and in about five minutes, five minutes, he told me I had Parkinson’s disease.

Five minutes. No brain scan. No imaging. No confirming test. A confident diagnosis, delivered with a firm handshake, dressed in a good suit, based on pattern-matching. My symptoms looked like the picture in his head, so that must be the picture.

Where have we heard that before tonight?

So I took the medication. Carbidopa. Levodopa. For six months. And it did nothing. No improvement. And only then, only after the drug had failed, only after six months of my life, did somebody finally order the scan. The DaTscan. The actual test. The evidence.

And the scan said I never had Parkinson’s at all.

I had spent six months, at eighty-one, being treated for a disease I did not have, because a very smart, very confident human being pattern-matched instead of verifying. And here is the line I wrote, and I want you to hear how it rhymes with everything else tonight: “just as large language models like ChatGPT are trained on vast datasets but lack judgment, so too has medicine become increasingly data-heavy and judgment-light.”

The machine that lies has a human cousin. And we all have an appointment with him.

“Healthcare cannot consume twenty percent of our economy with a twenty percent diagnostic error rate.” And do you know what happened to the neurologist who was confidently, expensively wrong about my brain? Nothing. “The neurologist who made the initial diagnosis suffered no consequence for the error.” No smell. No stain. Just a confident wrong answer, and an old man’s six months.

So when I tell you don’t lament, engage, I don’t mean it as a poster. I mean it as a survival strategy I have personally stress-tested. Verify. Ask again. Ask eight. Refuse the confident wrong answer, whether it comes from a machine or a man. And keep moving, keep wanting, keep playing, because the couch is more dangerous than the pickleball ever was.

Don’t lament. Engage.

[A wink.]

Even if you need both hands.

Movement Five: The Archaeology of Belief

Why we believe what we believe — ~11 minutes

[Settle. This one is a thinker. Slow it down.]

I want to try something with you now. I want to dig, gently, into the ground underneath your own convictions. Not to change them. Just to show you where they came from. Because I’ve come to believe that this is the most important thing an old man can hand to a young world, and almost nobody says it out loud.

Here is what fifty years and a lot of watching has taught me. “By age twelve, your political operating system is encoded.” By twelve. Before you could vote, before you could drive, before you had read a single serious book on any of it, the foundation was already poured. And everything after that, all the arguing, all the reading, all the certainty. Most of it is just decoration on a foundation you didn’t choose.

Let me show you what I mean. Let me introduce you to some Americans. I’ll make them up, but you’ll recognize every one.

[Build these as distinct people. Small shifts of voice, not caricature. Tender.]

Here is a man. He votes one way, fiercely, his whole life. Ask him why and he’ll give you a well-rehearsed speech about principles. But dig, and here’s what’s really at the bottom: when he was nine years old, men from the government came and audited his father’s small business, and he watched his father, his strong father, made small and afraid at the kitchen table. He didn’t decide his politics. His politics were installed the day he watched his father shrink.

Here is another. Votes the other way, just as fiercely. And at the bottom of his certainty: a father who gave twenty-two years to a company, to a plant, to a line, and got a watch. A watch, and a locked gate, and a pension that evaporated. The boy watched that too. He didn’t choose his convictions either. They were handed to him in the shape of his father’s betrayed loyalty.

Here is a family that watched the bank foreclose on the ranch next door, good people, hard workers, gone in a season, and a certain suspicion of institutions was born in that child at the mailbox, watching the neighbors load the truck.

Every one of them will tell you, with total sincerity, that they reasoned their way to their beliefs. Every one of them is standing on a foundation that was poured before they were tall enough to see over the kitchen counter.

I am not telling you this to make you cynical. I am telling you the opposite. I am telling you this so that the next time you are certain, bone-certain, righteously certain, that the person across from you is a fool or a villain, you might pause, just for a half-second, the way my foot pauses for the deer, and wonder what got poured into their foundation at nine years old.

Because here is what the machines have done to this ancient human problem. They have poured gasoline on it.

“The internet doesn’t change our minds: it mirrors them back, optimized for engagement.” That’s the whole trick. You think you’re searching for information. You’re not. “Every search is a mirror. Every feed is an echo. Every algorithm is grandmother’s wisdom at scale, optimized for engagement, not truth.” The machine watched what made you angry, what made you nod, what made you stay, and now it hands you more of exactly that, forever, and calls it the news.

We built a machine that finds the foundation that was poured when you were nine, and pours more concrete on it every single day, and charges admission.

And so I did the only experiment I know how to do. I took a genuinely divisive question, a hard one, a real one, and I put it to eight of these artificial minds. Eight independently-built systems, trained by different companies, in different ways.

And they didn’t reason. They mirrored. Each one reflected back the biases baked into whatever it was raised on, and reported it as objective fact, with no idea, none, where its own convictions came from. “When eight independently trained systems produce the same non-answer to a question about legitimacy, the uniformity itself is the data point.”

“The mirror didn’t lie,” I wrote. “It just showed us what we didn’t want to see.”

And what we didn’t want to see is this, and I’ll say it as gently as I can because it applies to me too, up here, an old man sure of his own clarity:

“Your convictions feel like your own. They may not be.”

[Long pause. Then, warm, releasing them from the discomfort.]

Now. If that’s true, and I’ve spent fifty years becoming sure that it is, then it changes what wisdom even means. Wisdom isn’t having the right foundation. Nobody chose their foundation. Wisdom is being the one person in the room willing to look down at your own, and say out loud: some of this was poured when I was nine, and I’d like to check it.

That’s the whole job. That’s what I’m trying to do, publicly, at eighty-three, in front of you. Check the foundation. Even mine. Especially mine.

Movement Six: What I Stopped Hiding

The emotional climax — ~15 minutes

[Everything slows now. This is the center of the evening. No performance. Just truth.]

I want to tell you about the thirtieth of December, two years ago.

I was driving. South Dakota, open road, the way I’ve told you I love. And a tire, a whole tire and wheel, come loose from some vehicle ahead of me, came at my car. And there was a collision, and suddenly I was stranded, alone, thirty miles from the nearest town, an old man on a cold road where being stranded is not an inconvenience. It can be the end.

And I didn’t die. I didn’t die because of infrastructure. Because systems I never think about, the car, the sensors, the automatic call for help, did their quiet work and reached out into the world on my behalf and said: a man is in trouble here. And help came.

I have paddled alone across oceans and I was nearly ended by a loose tire on a Tuesday. That’s the deal. That’s the whole deal, for all of us. You don’t get to know which Tuesday.

Now here is why I tell you that story. Almost exactly one year later, a year, to within days of that near-ending, my granddaughter gave birth. And I became a great-grandfather.

[Let it be simple. Let it be huge.]

One year. On one end of it, an old man nearly gone on a cold road. On the other, a brand-new human being, opening her eyes. And me, standing in the middle, in the little slot of time between the tire and the baby, apparently still here for a reason.

I wrote, around then, sitting near the ocean I love: “the information reached me faster than the next wave reached my feet.” Everything moves so fast now. Everything. Except the two things that have always moved at exactly the same speed they always have: a life ending, and a life beginning. “The ocean has all the time in the world.” And so, it turns out, do the only things that finally matter.

And it was that baby, that great-granddaughter, who made me do the bravest thing I have done in eighty-three years. Braver than the marathons. Braver than the oceans. Braver than founding a company at an age when I should have known better.

She made me stop hiding.

[Here it is. Steady. Don’t rush toward it. Arrive at it.]

Let me tell you about my parents. They were born in 1911. They were the children of refugees, people who had fled, with nothing, from a place that wanted them gone or dead. And when my parents raised me, they made a decision. A loving decision. A decision made entirely out of care, by people who had learned their caution in blood.

They gave me a name so that I could pass — Brian Demsey. It might have been Benyamin Danielovich.

A name that wouldn’t catch. A name that wouldn’t make anyone look too closely at where the family came from. They wanted their boy to move through the American century smooth and safe and unremarkable, and they built me a name like a good coat: plain, warm, and designed not to be noticed. “My parents chose it so I could pass.”

And it worked. For eighty years, it worked. I passed. I moved through rooms, boardrooms, mansions with polar bears in them, and nobody looked too closely, and I let them not look.

But I have lived a long time now. And I have watched, across eighty years, what silence actually purchases. And I am here to give you the actuarial finding, priced coldly by a man who priced things for a living:

“Silence doesn’t provide safety. It just means they come for you without anyone noticing.”

That’s the whole return on the investment of hiding. Nothing. The danger doesn’t leave. It just gets to arrive unannounced. My parents’ silence was love, and it was a bad trade, and I finally have the standing, the age, the clarity, the nothing-left-to-lose, to say so.

So when that baby was born, I sat down, and I wrote the sentence my family had not said out loud in three generations. And I’m going to say it to you now, in this room, out loud, because a thing you cannot say in the light is a thing that still owns you.

[Fully present. To them.]

My name is Brian Demsey. My parents chose it so that I could pass. I am eighty-three years old, a dreamer, not a programmer; a builder; a great-grandfather.

And I am Jewish.

[Let it land completely. Do not move. Let the silence be as long as it needs to be.]

Eighty years. It took me eighty years and a newborn to say those words in the light. That is what hiding costs. It doesn’t cost you your safety. It costs you your life, the open, unhidden living of it, and it charges you the bill right at the end, when you finally realize you spent the whole thing passing.

Now I want to tell you one more story, and then I’ll let this movement rest. Because I don’t want to leave you thinking this is only about my people, or only about fear. It’s about the opposite of hiding. It’s about the people who run toward.

There is a beach called Bondi, in Sydney, Australia. I first stood on it in 1984. And the men and women who guard that water, the lifesavers, the watermen, they have a tradition that goes back more than a hundred years. “The ocean taught me things no classroom ever could: patience, humility, the understanding that nature does not negotiate.” I learned that in the water myself. But the watermen learned something more.

Not long ago, on that beach, hatred came. An attack. The kind of thing my parents fled, arriving on the sunny sand of a country that was supposed to be far away from all that. People were hurt. People were killed. [Verify the specific names and details against your essay before speaking them: the victims, and the rescuers, by name.]

And here is what happened. Ordinary people ran toward the danger. And among the ones who ran toward it, who pulled the wounded from harm, who put their own bodies between the hatred and the hurt, were men who did not share the faith of the people they were saving. Men of one faith, running into the fire to save the children of another.

“That is the tradition of the watermen. They stand watch. They run toward danger. They do not ask who needs saving before they dive in.”

[Quiet. This is the moral center of the whole night.]

That’s it. That’s the answer to everything I’ve said tonight. The machine asks, “who is this person, what is their category, what does the data say about their kind?” The waterman asks nothing. The waterman dives in.

I spent eighty years hiding who I was, to be safe. And then I watched strangers run toward mortal danger to save people they’d never met and had every excuse to pass by. And I understood, finally, at eighty-three, that I had the safety equation exactly backwards my whole life.

You are not made safe by hiding what you are.

You are made safe by people willing to dive in for you, and by becoming, while there’s still time, one of the people who dives in.

Movement Seven: Going First

The resolution — ~14 minutes

[Lift. The evening turns from truth toward action. Warm, building, generous.]

So. What do you do with all this, if you’re not an eighty-three-year-old “haimascha face” with a lie-detector for robots and a secret he finally told?

Let me answer that with the best story I’ve got. Let me tell you how I once built a five-million-dollar institution with two and a half million dollars and a trick.

[Relish this. It’s a heist story, and you’re the thief.]

It was 1982. I had a friend, a physicist, Michael Berns, at the University of California, Irvine, and a dream that was much too big for the money in the room. We wanted to build something extraordinary. A laser institute. A place that would end up treating children, doing things nobody had done. And to build it, we needed to get to a great man named Dr. Arnold Beckman. Arnold Beckman, who had more or less invented the instruments modern science was built on, and who had the kind of fortune that could make a thing like this real.

And here was the problem, the problem every builder knows. Everybody wanted to give second. Everybody wanted to match. “I’m in, if the university’s in.” “We’ll commit, once Beckman commits.” “Show me the other money and I’ll add mine.” Everyone standing in a circle, wallet out, waiting for someone else to go first.

So I made the circle work for us. I went to each of them and I made their commitment contingent on the others. I structured it so that every “yes” unlocked the next “yes,” so that the university’s land was matched by Beckman’s money was matched by the commitment I already had. “I had satisfied everyone’s conditions by making their commitments circular.”

Nobody had to go first. So everybody went at once.

We built a five-million-dollar institute on two and a half million in cash. And the Beckman Laser Institute is real, and it has helped people, for decades, and it started as an old trick about who goes first.

[Now bring it home.]

I tell you that story for one reason. I did that in my forties, and everyone thought I was bold. And here is what nobody tells you, the great lie hiding underneath the polar bears and the twentysomething founders and the whole cult of youth we’ve built:

“It is never too late to be creative. It is never too late to be bold.”

“Silicon Valley worships at the altar of the twentysomething founder.” I know. I built a company at eighty-three; I’m familiar with the raised eyebrows. But I had fifty years of pattern recognition that no twenty-five-year-old could possibly have. The circular-funding trick that built the Beckman Institute: I’m running a version of it right now, at eighty-three, a ten-million-dollar matching ask for the work that matters to me now. The tool is the same. The boldness is the same. I just know more.

So here is what I want to leave you with. And I want to be careful, because you came for an evening with an old man and I don’t want to send you home with a slogan. I want to send you home with a job.

Most of us are waiting for a hero. Be honest. We are. We are waiting for the leader who’ll fix it, the founder who’ll save it, the president who’ll reunite us, the genius who’ll un-break the world. “The hero isn’t coming. There’s no JFK waiting in the wings, no Reagan who’ll reunite us, no Obama who promised to transcend the division.”

Nobody is coming. I’m eighty-three; I’ve waited through a lot of “somebody will fix this,” and I can report, from the far end of the wait: nobody is coming.

Which sounds like the bad news. It is actually the only good news there is. Because if nobody is coming, then the only thing left, the only thing that has ever actually worked, is the hardest and simplest thing in the world. “Going first.”

Going first. Being the one who says the true thing before it’s safe. Being the one who checks their own foundation out loud. Being the one who tells on himself first, and earns the right to tell the truth about everything else. Being the waterman who dives in without asking who’s drowning. Being the one who makes the circle work by refusing to wait for someone else to commit.

“Someone has to break the cycle. Why not you?”

I built a machine that made me nothing and taught me everything. I hid for eighty years and then I stopped. I was nearly ended by a tire and then I held my great-granddaughter. And I stand here tonight, twelve stents and twenty-two marathons and one very good secret finally told, to give you the only wisdom I’ve earned that I’m sure is true.

The world does not owe you relevance. “You have to claim it.” At any age. Especially at mine. “The secret isn’t graceful acceptance. The secret is defiant engagement,” on behalf of the ones who come after, the great-grandchildren, the baby who will be nine years old someday soon, watching you at the kitchen table, learning from your face what to pour into their foundation.

They once asked me what I’d want for an epitaph. Here’s the truth of it: that “in some crazy way, with no more than average skills and a lot of luck,” I “stimulated people to have an opportunity to help themselves and improve their lives.” That’s all. Average skills, a lot of luck, and the refusal to sit down.

That’s available to you. All of it. Tonight.

So look at me one last time. What do you see?

Not a frail old man. I hope, by now, you see something more useful. You see a rear-view mirror that learned to watch the road. You see a man who lost the investment and kept the education and would make the trade again tomorrow. You see the clarity that’s waiting for you on the far side of all the noise, if you’re lucky enough and stubborn enough to get there.

And you see somebody who came all this way, to this room, to tell you the words that matter even more than my own name.

It is later than you think.
And there is more time than you fear.

Both at once. Both true.

Don’t lament.

Engage.

[Hold. Then, simply:]

Thank you. Good night.
